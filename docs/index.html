Quantization and digit recall · Phases 1–3

Qwen Digit Recall

Compressing a language model makes it smaller and faster. This study checks what that does to answers that hinge on one exact token, like the 327th digit of π. 64 versions of three Qwen models, from the uncompressed original down to 2.1 bits per weight, were asked to recite π from memory, copy digits from the prompt, and choose between the two.

Kiryl Dubarenka · llama.cpp bed0a8566 · SYCL · Arc Pro B60 · greedy decoding · 6,600 scored requests + 256 teacher-forced passes

Before you start

How to read this page

Bits per weight (bpw)
How many bits each of the model's numbers takes in the file, measured as file size × 8 ÷ number of weights. The original is 16. Fewer bits means a smaller file and more rounding error.
BF16, Q8_0, Q6_K … Q2_K
llama.cpp's format names. BF16 is the uncompressed original. In the others the number is roughly the bits per weight. K marks the newer K-quant family, and _S/_M/_L/_XL is a size within a format. Q4_0 is an older format. IQ formats (IQ4_XS, IQ3_XXS, IQ2_XXS) squeeze harder through lookup tables.
us_, UD-, _imat
us_ means a file published by unsloth. No prefix means built here. UD- is unsloth's "Dynamic" recipe, with a different precision for each layer. _imat means built with an imatrix, a record of which weights matter most on a sample text, so compression can protect them. Ours was made from Wikipedia text with all numbers removed.
Reference
The version everything is compared against: BF16 for the 4B and 9B, Q8_0 for the 27B (its BF16 doesn't fit on two 24 GB cards).
Plain ladder
Our own builds of Q8_0 down to Q2_K, with no imatrix. Only the bit width changes, so it is the cleanest comparison.
The tests
E1 recites π, e and √2 from memory, in three wordings. The score is the number of digits before the first mistake, and figures show the middle of the three. E2 (teacher forcing) feeds the model the true digits and records how sure it was of each one. E3 copies random digits from the prompt. E4b writes digits out as words. E5 copies π or e with one digit deliberately changed. E6 recites π as words.
Odds (how sure)
How many times more likely the right digit is than the runner-up. 1× is a dead heat, 4× is shaky, 10,000× is certain. Technical note: this is e raised to the log-probability margin in nats.
Noise
How far a compressed version's predictions drift from the reference's, averaged over every digit (the KL divergence). In this study, 0.0001 gave the same recitations as the reference, about 0.01 made π recall stop within the first few hundred digits, about 0.1 cut it to around 100, and 1 or more meant a broken model. "On π" means measured on π's digits (memory). "On random" means on random digits that nobody can have memorized (generic damage).
Log scale
Noise and odds are plotted on log scales, where each step up the axis multiplies the value by 10 (or 100, where labelled). A point one gridline lower on a noise chart has a tenth of the noise.
Copy errors
A substitution is a wrong digit, an insertion an extra one, a deletion a missing one. A run is a stretch of repeated digits, like 8888.
Speed and memory
Decode is how fast the model writes, in tokens per second. Prompt speed is how fast it reads a 4,096-token prompt. VRAM is graphics memory used, in GiB (1 GiB = 1.07 GB).

H5 · model size

A bigger model helps only at very low bits

The same plain ladder, built the same way, on three models. The "cliff" is the most-bits build whose π recall falls below half of the reference's: Q5_K_M (5.9 bits per weight) on the 4B, Q3_K_M (4.1) on the 9B and Q4_K_M (4.9) on the 27B. The 27B also comes from a newer generation (Qwen 3.8), so size isn't the only thing that changes.

  • At 5–6 bits the 27B is no quieter than the 9B. Both sit at a noise of 0.0001–0.0004 on π, well below the 4B (0.001–0.008).
  • From 4 bits down the 27B pulls ahead. At Q3_K_M its noise on π is 3.5 times lower than the 9B's. At Q2_K it is 4 times lower than both smaller models, which end up level there. Its π recall at Q3_K_M (271) and Q2_K (58) is the best of the three.
  • The cliff follows the uncertain digits. The 27B's Q4_K_M has the 9B's level of noise, yet it first breaks at digit 463, where the reference was only 18 times surer of the right digit than the runner-up. The 27B has 6 digits under 20×, the 9B 2.
  • Per byte, the order reverses. The 4B at Q8_0 recites 1,000 digits from 4.5 GB, the 9B at Q6_K 1,056 from 7.4 GB, and the 27B needs 19.5 GB for 1,046. Exact recall needs many bits per weight more than it needs many weights.
Phase 3 · Qwen 3.8 27B

The 27B: knows less π, holds it longer

17 versions: unsloth's Q8_0 as the reference, 10 more unsloth files, our plain ladder from Q6_K to Q2_K, and Q2_K with our imatrix. The three largest files run split across both cards.

  • A limit of memory, unrelated to compression. Q8_0 stops at digit 1,046 of π in two wordings. After …2796823030 it writes 8979… where π goes on 1952…. Every build down to 5.1 bits stops at the same digit. The 9B gets past it, so the bigger, newer model has memorized less π.
  • Certainty predicts the steps. π recall moves in steps (990, 463, 437, 271, 167, 163), and each is the first digit that build gets wrong under teacher forcing. The typical stop sits at the 2nd percentile of Q8_0's certainty.
  • The recipe matters below 5 bits. unsloth's UD-Q4_K_M has half the noise on π of our Q4_K_M and recites 990 digits against 463. At 5–6 bits the builds are level.
  • Copying is the strongest of the three models. Plain Q2_K copies 17,000 digits without an error. Only UD-IQ2_XXS (2.1 bits) drops digits. Every build copies all 8 planted digits.
  • Reciting as words works. Asked for π in English words, Q8_0 is right for 46 digits ("one four one five nine…"). The smaller models produced fragments.

Phase 2 · Qwen 3.5 9B

The 9B: no near-ties, sturdier copying

19 versions: unsloth's BF16 reference and 12 published files, our plain ladder from Q6_K to Q2_K, and Q2_K with our imatrix.

  • Certainty. BF16's least certain digit of π is still 5 times likelier than the runner-up, against 1.1 times on the 4B. Certainty also grows deeper into π, from about 700× over the first 100 digits to about 70,000× after digit 300. Recall still breaks on less certain digits: unsloth's Q4_K_M, UD-Q4_K_XL and Q4_0 all first go wrong at digit 722 (6th percentile).
  • Copying. Every build from 3.4 bits per weight up copies at 0.03–0.07 errors per 100 digits, except plain Q2_K (34). The 4B's habit of repeating words in E4b at 3.6–4.3 bpw is gone. Only UD-IQ2_XXS still does it.
  • Prompt against memory. The 9B's BF16 copies all 8 planted digits faithfully, and so does every build that can copy at all. Memory doesn't pull against the prompt, so compression has nothing to change.
  • e and √2. Both models know exactly 192 digits of e when asked plainly. The 9B reads "e to 1000 decimal places: 2." as a numbered list and "e = 2." as Python code.

Phase 1 · 4B · H1 · free recall

Recall declines early and steadily

The sections below cover the 4B in detail. BF16 recites 1,000 correct digits of π and stops there, because the prompt asks for 1,000. It knows 192 digits of e and 105 of √2. Only π gives enough room to watch recall shrink, so it carries H1. Every standard build below Q8_0 loses digits, and there is no safe zone down to 4 bits. Recall falls below half of BF16's at Q5_K_M, 5.9 bits per weight.

Phase 1 · 4B · H2 · how sure the model is

Errors start at a few near-tie digits

Feed the model the true digits one at a time, and at each position you can see how sure it was: how many times more likely the right digit was than the runner-up. BF16 gets all 1,000 positions right, and for a typical digit the right answer is about 10,000 times likelier. A handful of digits are nearly tied, though, and recall breaks there first.

  • Of 77 π recitals that stopped early, the most common stopping points are positions 326 (8 times), 88 (7), and 821, 10, 87 and 123 (4 each). The typical stop sits at the 12th percentile of BF16's certainty.
  • With few bits removed, only the most uncertain digits flip. Q6_K's two errors under teacher forcing are at 813 and 821. At Q4_K_M the 14 errors sit around the 7.6th percentile. From Q3_K_M down the noise overwhelms most digits, and errors appear everywhere.
  • This also explains why neighbouring builds can score so differently. Our Q6_K recites 813 digits and unsloth's Q6_K 326, with almost the same noise. Both are balanced on digit 326, and one gets past it.
Phase 1 · 4B · H3 · copying from the prompt

Copying holds, and it fails by skipping digits in runs

Copying digits (E3). 40 random strings of 100 to 1,000 digits. BF16 makes 3 errors in 17,000 digits. Every build at 4.3 bits per weight or more copies the 1,000-digit strings perfectly. Only three builds break, and the same formats work when built differently:

Writing digits as words (E4b). Even BF16 drops digits, always inside runs. Strings with many runs get 2.2 errors per 100 digits, against 0.3 on ordinary random ones. 75 of BF16's 80 errors are a missing word inside a run of three or more equal digits: "eight eight eight eight eight" written one short. That stays the same down to about 4.3 bits per weight. Below it the failures become extra words, as the model gets stuck repeating one. Wrong digits stay rare except in builds that collapse completely.

The first version of this prompt (E4, "write each digit as an English word") failed as a test. The model read it as "say this number" and answered "twenty trillion two hundred…", even at BF16. Those records are kept. E4b adds one example ("507 becomes: five zero seven") and replaces it in the results.

Phase 1 · 4B · H4 · format, imatrix, recipe

The recipe matters more than the sample text

Each format was built several ways: a standard build with no imatrix, with our number-free imatrix, with unsloth's imatrix, and unsloth's published file.

  • Format. At the same size, the older Q4_0 has 3.3 times the noise on π of Q4_K_M.
  • Imatrix. Ours comes from text with no digit strings. It halves generic noise at Q4_K_M and does nothing for π, where the noise even rises. An imatrix protects what its sample text uses. At 2–3 bits it matters for everything: it separates a Q2_K that can't copy from one that can.
  • Recipe. A standard Q4_K_M built with unsloth's imatrix gets little of unsloth's advantage (noise on π 0.041 against 0.022). Most of the gain comes from how they choose precision for each part of the model. Q3_K_M shows the same: 0.29 with their imatrix, 0.17 with their file.
Phase 1 · 4B · H6 · prompt against memory

Compression weakens memory's pull

E5 asks for an exact copy of π or e with one digit changed. Only position 10 of π separates the versions. BF16 and almost every build at 4.6 bits per weight or more "corrects" it back to the real digit of π. Almost every build at 4.4 or below copies the prompt faithfully. That is the opposite of H6, and it fits H1: compression wears away memorized content, so memory competes less with the prompt.

Phase 1 · 4B · Resources and speed

What it takes to run each build

One Arc Pro B60 (24 GB), llama.cpp with SYCL, room for 8,192 tokens of conversation. Writing speed is the middle value the server reported on the real test requests, within 0–5% of llama.cpp's own benchmark. Prompt speed comes from the benchmark at 4,096 tokens, because short prompts carry a fixed overhead on this backend that hides the real throughput.

  • Below about 5 bits, smaller files stop getting faster. Writing goes 38 → 65 → 89 tokens/s from BF16 to Q8_0 to Q4_K_M, then stays flat down to Q2_K. Past that point a smaller file of a 4B model saves memory and nothing else.
  • IQ formats are slow on this backend. Unpacking their lookup tables costs more time than the smaller file saves. UD-XL files write 12–28% slower than plain K-quants of a similar size.

The setup checks passed for every model: identical bytes on repeated requests and after a server restart, and for the 4B on both cards. Our Q8_0 and unsloth's Q8_0 of the 4B agree to a noise of 0.00013, so the pipeline adds no error of its own.
