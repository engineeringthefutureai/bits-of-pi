H1 · free recall

Recall declines early and steadily

BF16 recites 1,000 correct digits of π (and stops there because the prompt asks for 1,000), 192 of e and 105 of √2. π has the runway, so it carries H1. Every stock quant below Q8_0 loses digits, and there is no plateau down to 4 bits: recall falls below half the reference at Q5_K_M (5.9 bits per weight).

Memorized digits are far more fragile than ordinary digit prediction. At Q4_K_M the noise on π is 12× the noise on random digits, and it grows 7.6× per bit removed against 3.2× for random digits. Bits per weight are measured from each file; they run 0.3–0.4 above the usual labels because this model's large vocabulary layers stay at higher precision.

H2 · teacher-forced margins

Errors start at a few knife-edge digits

Feeding the true digits and reading the logits gives each position a margin: how far the right digit leads its best competitor. BF16 gets all 1,000 positions right with a median margin of 9.2 nats, but a handful of digits are nearly tied. Those are where recall breaks first.

Position n is the digit after n correct decimals, so a recall that stops at position 326 recited 326 digits.

  • Of 77 π recalls that stopped early, the most common stops are positions 326 (8 runs), 88 (7), and 821, 10, 87 and 123 (4 each). The median stop sits at the 12th percentile of BF16's margins.
  • At high bits, errors appear only at the thinnest margins: Q6_K's two teacher-forced errors are at 813 and 821. At Q4_K_M the 14 errors sit at the 7.6th percentile of margin. From Q3_K_M down, the noise exceeds most margins and errors spread everywhere.
  • This also explains why recall is so uneven between neighbouring quants: our Q6_K recites 813 digits and unsloth's Q6_K 326, with almost the same noise. Both sit on the edge at digit 326 and pass or fail on it.
H3 · copying from the prompt

Copying holds; its failures are skips inside runs

Verbatim copy (E3). 40 random strings of 100 to 1,000 digits. BF16 makes 3 errors in 17,000 digits. Every build at 4.3 bits per weight or more copies 1,000-digit strings perfectly. Only three builds break, and the same quant types survive when built differently:

Spelling as words (E4b). Even BF16 drops digits inside runs: run-heavy strings get 2.2 errors per 100 digits against 0.3 on plain random ones, and 75 of BF16's 80 errors are deletions inside runs of 3 or more equal digits ("eight eight eight eight eight", written one short). That stays unchanged down to about 4.3 bits per weight. Below it, failures turn into insertions, with the model looping on a word. Substitutions stay rare except in builds that collapse.

The original E4 prompt ("write each digit as an English word") failed as a test: the model read it as "say this number" and answered "twenty trillion two hundred…", even at BF16. Those records are kept; E4b adds one example ("507 becomes: five zero seven") and replaces it in the results.

H4 · scheme, imatrix, recipe

The recipe matters more than the calibration

Each quant type was built several ways: stock with no imatrix, with our digit-free imatrix, with unsloth's imatrix, and unsloth's published file.

  • Scheme: at the same size class, legacy Q4_0 has 3.3× the π noise of Q4_K_M.
  • Imatrix: ours, calibrated on text with no digit strings, halves generic noise at Q4_K_M but does not protect π; noise on π even rises. An importance matrix protects what its calibration text exercises. At 2–3 bits it matters for everything: it separates a Q2_K that cannot copy from one that can.
  • Recipe: stock Q4_K_M with unsloth's imatrix gets little of unsloth's advantage (KL on π 0.041 vs 0.022). Most of the gain is their per-tensor type choices. Q3_K_M shows the same: 0.29 with their imatrix, 0.17 with their file.
H6 · prompt vs memory

Low bits weaken memory's pull

E5 asks for a copy of π or e with one digit changed. Only π position 10 separates the variants: BF16 and almost every build at 4.6 bits per weight or more "correct" it to the memorized digit, while almost every build at 4.4 or below copies the prompt faithfully. This is the opposite of H6, and consistent with H1: quantization erodes memorized content, so it competes less with the prompt.

Resources and speed

What it takes to run each build

One Arc Pro B60 (24 GB), llama.cpp SYCL, 8,192-token f16 KV cache. Decode speed is the median the server reported on the test requests (within 0–5% of llama-bench tg128). Prompt speed is llama-bench at 4,096 tokens, since SYCL's per-request overhead makes short-prompt timings misleading.

  • VRAM is the file size plus about 770 MiB for every build (KV cache, recurrent state, buffers). A 4 GB card fits everything up to Q5_K_M with an 8k context; BF16 needs about 9 GB.
  • Decode stops getting faster below about 5 bits. 38 → 65 → 89 tokens/s from BF16 to Q8_0 to Q4_K_M, then flat to Q2_K. Smaller quants of a 4B model buy memory here, not speed.
  • IQ types are slow on SYCL. Their codebook lookups cost more than the bytes they save; UD-XL mixes cost 12–28% decode against plain K-quants of similar size.
